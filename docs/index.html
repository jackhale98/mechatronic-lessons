The kit

  • A dev board with hardware encoder timers. An STM32 Nucleo-class board is ideal. An Arduino-class board is fine for the early projects.

Work in rad/s internally and convert at the boundary, because datasheets mix units freely. 1000 rpm is 104.7 rad/s. 1 kg·cm² is 10⁻⁴ kg·m². 1 oz·in is 7.06 mN·m, and 1 lb·in is 0.113 N·m.

Inertias you will actually compute

  • Solid disk or shaft about its axis: J = ½·m·r².
  • Thin ring: J = m·r². A thick tube: J = ½·m·(ro² + ri²).
  • Parallel axis: J = Jc + m·d² for a mass offset by d from the rotation axis.
  • Linear mass on a belt or rack with pulley radius r: J = m·r², seen at the pulley shaft.
  • Linear mass on a screw with lead p (meters of travel per revolution): J = m·(p/2π)².

The last two convert a linear load into an equivalent rotary inertia at the shaft that drives it. Module 10 adds the gear ratio, which divides the load inertia seen by the motor by N².

Rule of thumb

For a disk of fixed thickness, mass scales with r² and inertia with m·r², so J grows with r⁴. Doubling a pulley's diameter can multiply its inertia by 16. Hollow out or shrink rotating parts before you upsize the motor.

The torque budget

The motor has to supply every torque on the drive train at once: T = Jtotal·α + b·ω + Tf + Tload. Coulomb friction Tf is roughly constant (bearings, seals, guide rails). Viscous friction b·ω grows with speed (lubricant shear, eddy-current losses). Tload is the process itself: gravity on a vertical axis, a cutting force, a spring. Acceleration torque dominates fast indexing moves. Friction and gravity dominate slow, heavy ones.

Power and energy

Mechanical power is P = T·ω. A gearbox trades torque for speed but never adds power, and every stage loses some. The kinetic energy ½·J·ω² stored in a moving system has to go somewhere when it stops: into friction, a mechanical brake, a brake resistor, or back into the power supply as regenerated energy. Modules 05 and 14 deal with that last case.

Sensors, drivers and controllers are built from a handful of laws. Most puzzling bench behavior, like a sensor reading that sags, a resistor that runs hot, or a signal that changes when you clip on the scope, traces back to Ohm's law and loading.

The three quantities

  • Voltage V is a potential difference between two points, the electrical “effort”. It is always measured between two nodes. Ground is simply the node you decide to call 0 V.
  • Current I is the flow of charge, in amps. It always returns to its source, and the return path matters as much as the outgoing wire (Module 14).
  • Resistance R relates them: V = I·R. Power dissipated is P = V·I = I²·R = V²/R. Small through-hole and SMD resistors are usually rated 0.1–0.25 W.

Kirchhoff's laws

KVL: the voltages around any closed loop sum to zero. KCL: the currents into any node sum to zero. Series resistances add. Parallel resistances combine as 1/R = Σ 1/Ri, which for two is R1R2/(R1+R2), written R1‖R2.

Mechanical bridge

A series RLC circuit is a mass–spring–damper. Its natural frequency is ωn = 1/√(LC) and its damping ratio is ζ = (R/2)·√(C/L). Everything you know about vibration transfers directly, including resonance peaks and ringing.

Dividers, Thévenin and loading

A voltage divider gives Vout = Vin·R2/(R1+R2), but only with nothing connected to its output. Any linear source, seen from its output terminals, behaves like an ideal voltage Vth in series with a resistance Rth. For the divider, Rth = R1‖R2. Connect a load RL and it forms a second divider with Rth, so the output drops by roughly Rth/(Rth+RL).

Rule of thumb

Keep the load at least 10× the source resistance for about 10% error, 100× for about 1%. ADC inputs charge an internal sampling capacitor, so many datasheets ask for a source impedance below roughly 10 kΩ, or a buffer amplifier in front.

Op-amps in two rules

With negative feedback, treat an op-amp as ideal: its inputs draw no current, and its output does whatever it must to make the two input voltages equal. That gives the building blocks you'll use most:

  • Voltage follower (buffer): gain 1, very high input impedance, low output impedance. It cures loading.
  • Non-inverting amplifier: gain 1 + Rf/Rg.
  • Inverting amplifier: gain −Rf/Rin.
  • Difference and instrumentation amplifiers: amplify the difference between two inputs while rejecting what they share. Used for bridges and current shunts (Module 07).

Real parts can't swing past their supply rails (use “rail-to-rail” parts on 3.3 V systems), have a finite gain–bandwidth product, and add a small offset voltage.

Motor windings, solenoids and relay coils are inductors. Decoupling, filtering and timing use capacitors. Their time constants decide how fast current builds in a coil and how much noise reaches your ADC.

Capacitors

A capacitor's current is proportional to how fast its voltage changes: i = C·dv/dt. Its voltage can't jump, because that would take infinite current. It stores ½·C·V². You'll use them for decoupling (a 100 nF ceramic at every IC supply pin, plus bulk electrolytic capacitance next to motor drivers), filtering, debouncing and timing.

Practical notes: ceramic X7R capacitors lose a large share of their capacitance under DC bias; electrolytics are polarized and have series resistance (ESR) that heats them under ripple current. Rate voltage at 1.5–2× the working value.

Inductors

An inductor's voltage is proportional to how fast its current changes: v = L·di/dt. Current can't jump, so if you force it to stop quickly the voltage spikes. That is the flyback problem in Module 04. It stores ½·L·I². Motor windings typically measure 0.1–10 Ω and 0.1–10 mH. Iron-cored inductors saturate above a rated current, after which their inductance collapses.

First-order response

Any circuit with one energy-storage element responds exponentially with a time constant τ = RC or τ = L/R. It reaches 63% at 1τ, 86% at 2τ, 95% at 3τ and 99.3% at 5τ. A motor's electrical time constant τe = L/R sets how quickly its current, and therefore its torque, can change.

Mechanical bridge

An RC circuit charging is a spring and damper with no mass: the position creeps to equilibrium with τ = c/k. An RL circuit is a mass pushed by a constant force against viscous drag: velocity rises with τ = m/c. Same equation, same curve.

RC low-pass filters

A series R with a C to ground passes slow signals and attenuates fast ones. The corner frequency is fc = 1/(2πRC). At fc the output is 0.707 of the input (−3 dB) and lags by 45°. Above it, amplitude falls 20 dB per decade and the lag approaches 90°. Two stages in cascade fall 40 dB per decade.

Use them to clean up a potentiometer or analog sensor and as an anti-aliasing filter ahead of an ADC (Module 06). Every filter also adds delay. Well below fc, an RC filter behaves roughly like a pure delay of RC seconds.

Rule of thumb

Put a filter's corner at least 5–10× above the bandwidth of any control loop that uses the filtered signal, or its phase lag will eat into your phase margin (Module 13).

A microcontroller pin can supply roughly 10–20 mA at 3.3 V. A motor or solenoid wants amps at 12–48 V. Diodes and transistors are the valves between them, and the H-bridge is the arrangement that lets one supply drive a motor in both directions.

Diodes

A diode is a check valve. It conducts forward with a drop of about 0.6–0.7 V (silicon) or 0.2–0.4 V (Schottky) and blocks reverse voltage up to its rating. Rectifier and Schottky diodes are used for flyback paths and reverse-polarity protection. Zener diodes hold a set reverse voltage. TVS diodes absorb fast transients on power and signal lines.

Transistors as switches

BJTs are current-controlled: collector current is about β times base current. Driven hard (saturated), a BJT drops about 0.2 V. Good for relays, LEDs and small loads.

MOSFETs are voltage-controlled. The gate is an insulated capacitor; once the gate–source voltage is well above threshold, the channel acts as a small resistance Rds(on). The threshold Vgs(th) is where it barely starts to conduct, so for direct MCU drive choose “logic-level” parts with Rds(on) specified at Vgs = 4.5 V or lower. Losses come from conduction (I²·Rds(on)) and from each switching transition, which grows with frequency. Charging the gate quickly takes amps of peak current, which is what gate-driver ICs provide.

An N-channel MOSFET between the load and ground (low-side) is the simplest switch. A high-side N-channel needs a gate voltage above the supply, which drivers generate with a bootstrap capacitor or charge pump. P-channel parts avoid that but have higher resistance.

Inductive loads need an escape path

When a switch opens on a coil, the coil's current keeps flowing and drives the voltage up until something conducts. Without a flyback path, that something is the MOSFET breaking down in avalanche, every cycle. A diode across the coil clamps the switch to Vs + 0.7 V and lets the current circulate and decay slowly. A Zener in series with that diode clamps higher, so the current decays faster. That matters for relays and solenoids, which release late with a plain diode.

Dropping a motor's voltage with a linear transistor burns the difference as heat. Switching fully on and off at high frequency, and letting the winding inductance smooth the current, gives variable voltage at 90–98% efficiency.

Duty cycle and average voltage

Pulse-width modulation switches the supply Vs on for a fraction D of each period T = 1/f. The average voltage is D·Vs. The motor responds to the average because both its electrical time constant (L/R) and its mechanical time constant are much longer than the PWM period.

Mechanical bridge

A flywheel smooths the impulses of a piston engine. Winding inductance does the same for current: the larger L is relative to the switching period, the smoother the current.

Current ripple

During the on-time the current ramps up, during the off-time it ramps down. In steady state the peak-to-peak ripple is approximately ΔI = Vs·D·(1−D)/(L·f), worst at 50% duty. Ripple adds I²R heating without adding average torque, causes torque ripple, and is the source of PWM whine.

Choosing the switching frequency

  • 16–40 kHz is typical for small motors: above hearing, with manageable switching losses.
  • Higher frequency means less ripple but more switching loss in the transistors. Large industrial drives often run 4–16 kHz.
  • Low-inductance motors (coreless DC, some BLDC) need higher frequencies or added series inductance.

Quadrants and regeneration

A single low-side switch with a freewheel diode drives one direction and can't actively brake (one quadrant). A half-bridge adds braking in one direction. A full H-bridge can drive and brake in both directions (four quadrants).

When a load decelerates, the motor becomes a generator and pushes current back into the supply. Bus capacitors charge up, and many bench supplies can't absorb current, so the bus voltage rises. Large bus capacitance, a brake resistor switched across the bus, or a clamp prevents an overvoltage trip. Module 14 has a calculator for this.

The same PWM idea plus an LC filter is a buck converter, the DC-DC regulator found on nearly every motor-control board.

The pattern for motion control: let hardware handle everything timing-critical (PWM edges, encoder counting, ADC triggering) and run the control math in a timer interrupt at a fixed rate. Avoid blocking delays in the loop.

ADC resolution

An N-bit converter with reference Vref resolves steps of one LSB = Vref/2N. A 12-bit ADC on 3.3 V resolves 0.81 mV. The ideal signal-to-noise ratio is 6.02·N + 1.76 dB; real converters lose 1–2 effective bits (ENOB) to noise. Amplify small signals so they use most of the input range.

Sampling and aliasing

Sampling at fs can only represent frequencies below fs/2 (the Nyquist frequency). Anything above folds down and appears at falias = |f − k·fs| for the nearest integer k. Once aliased, a signal can't be separated digitally, so the fix is an analog anti-alias filter in front of the ADC.

Mechanical bridge

Aliasing is the stroboscope effect. A spinning fan under a strobe that flashes just faster than its rotation rate appears to turn slowly backward.

Rule of thumb

Sample a control loop 10–20× faster than its desired closed-loop bandwidth. Typical rates: current loops 10–40 kHz, velocity loops 1–10 kHz, position loops 0.5–5 kHz.

Simple digital filters

A moving average over N samples delays the signal by (N−1)/2 samples. A first-order IIR filter, y ← y + α·(x − y) with α = 1 − e−2π·fc/fs, is the digital twin of an RC filter. It uses one line of code and one state variable.

You can only control what you measure. A sensor's resolution, noise and latency set the floor on how well the whole system can perform.

The vocabulary

Resolution is the smallest change the sensor reports. Accuracy is how close the reading is to the truth. Repeatability is how consistently it returns the same reading for the same input. A 20-bit encoder mounted on a wobbly shaft has fine resolution and poor accuracy. Also check range, bandwidth, latency and noise.

Mechanical bridge

Resolution versus accuracy is the same distinction as a caliper's display digits versus its calibrated tolerance.

Position

  • Incremental quadrature encoders output two square waves, A and B, a quarter cycle apart. Which one leads tells direction. Counting every edge of both (4× decoding) gives 4 counts per line (PPR = pulses per revolution). An index pulse Z marks one point per revolution for homing. Optical and magnetic versions are common.
  • Absolute encoders report position directly (single- or multi-turn) over SPI, SSI or BiSS, so no homing is needed at power-up.
  • Resolvers are rugged rotary transformers with analog outputs; they survive heat and shock.
  • Potentiometers are cheap and absolute but wear and are noisy.
  • Hall sensors in BLDC motors give coarse rotor position for commutation (Module 09).
  • LVDTs and linear scales measure linear position directly at the load, bypassing transmission errors.

Velocity from an encoder

Differencing counts over a fixed time gives velocity, but at low speed only a few counts arrive per sample, so the estimate is coarse and noisy. Options: a longer window (adds delay), timing the interval between edges, or a filter or observer. This trade between noise and delay comes up again in Module 12.

Force, torque and strain

A strain gauge's resistance changes by ΔR/R = GF·ε, where the gauge factor GF ≈ 2 and ε is strain. That change is tiny (0.1% at 500 µε), so gauges sit in a Wheatstone bridge, which outputs only the imbalance. A quarter bridge (one active gauge) outputs about Vex·GF·ε/4. A full bridge with four active gauges gives 4× the signal and cancels temperature drift. Load cells and torque transducers are full bridges rated in mV/V. The output, a few millivolts, needs an instrumentation amplifier before the ADC.

Current, inertial, proximity and temperature

  • Current: a low-value shunt resistor with a current-sense amplifier, or an isolated Hall-effect sensor. Motor current is proportional to torque, so current sensing doubles as a torque estimate.
  • Inertial: MEMS accelerometers and gyros (IMUs). Gyros drift and accelerometers are noisy, so they are fused in software.
  • Proximity and limit: mechanical switches, inductive proximity sensors (industrial 24 V, NPN or PNP outputs), optical interrupters. Used for homing and end-of-travel safety.
  • Temperature: NTC thermistors (cheap, nonlinear), RTDs (accurate), thermocouples (high temperature, need cold-junction compensation).

The brushed DC motor is the cleanest model of electromechanical energy conversion. Brushless motors behave the same way once their commutation is handled electronically, so this model carries through the rest of the course.

Two coupled equations

The electrical side: the applied voltage drives current through the winding resistance and inductance, against a back-EMF proportional to speed. The mechanical side: torque proportional to current accelerates the rotor against friction and load.

The motor model
V = R·i + L·di/dt + Ke·ωElectrical: voltage balance in the winding

In SI units the torque constant Kt (N·m/A) and the back-EMF constant Ke (V·s/rad) are the same number. That follows from energy conservation: the electrical power converted, Ke·ω·i, equals the mechanical power produced, Kt·i·ω. Spin a motor with a drill and measure the open-circuit voltage, and you've measured its torque constant.

The torque–speed line

In steady state the equations reduce to a straight line: ω = (V − R·T/Kt)/Ke. It runs from no-load speed ω0 = V/Ke to stall torque Tstall = Kt·V/R. Raising voltage shifts the line outward in parallel.

  • Maximum power is at half stall torque and half no-load speed: Pmax = V²/(4R) (ignoring friction). Efficiency there is under 50%.
  • Maximum efficiency is at low torque, where the current is √(I0·Istall). Continuous operation belongs on the high-speed side of the curve.

Reading a datasheet

  • Kv (rpm/V), common for hobby BLDC motors: Ke = 60/(2π·Kv) = 9.549/Kv.
  • Terminal resistance and inductance give stall current and τe = L/R.
  • No-load current I0 captures internal friction: Tf ≈ Kt·I0.
  • Rated (continuous) torque is a thermal limit: I²R heating the winding. Peak torque is limited by magnet demagnetization and the driver, and is allowed only briefly.
  • Rotor inertia goes into every acceleration calculation (Module 15).
Rule of thumb

Copper resistance rises about 0.39% per °C. A winding at 100 °C has roughly 30% more resistance than at 25 °C, so stall torque and efficiency drop as the motor warms up.

Two time constants

The electrical time constant τe = L/R (typically 0.1–5 ms) sets how fast current and torque respond. The mechanical time constant τm = J·R/(KtKe) (typically 5–100 ms) sets how fast an unloaded motor reaches speed after a voltage step. Usually τm ≫ τe, which is why cascaded control (Module 13) works so well.

In practice you'll choose from brushed DC, brushless (BLDC and PMSM), steppers and AC induction motors, often bought as a packaged servo with a drive. They trade cost, torque density, control complexity and precision.

Commutation is the whole story

Torque comes from the stator's magnetic field pulling on the rotor's field, and it is proportional to the sine of the angle between them. It is greatest when they are 90° apart. Keeping them near 90° as the rotor turns is called commutation. A brushed motor does it mechanically with brushes and a commutator. A brushless motor does it electronically, which requires knowing rotor position.

Brushless: six-step versus field-oriented control

Six-step (trapezoidal) commutation uses three Hall sensors that divide each electrical revolution into six sectors. In each sector, two phases are energized, and the stator field jumps 60° at each sector boundary. The angle between fields swings between 60° and 120°, so torque varies between sin 60° = 0.87 and 1. That 13% ripple is acceptable for fans, pumps and drones, and the scheme is simple.

Field-oriented control (FOC) measures phase currents, transforms them into a frame rotating with the rotor (Clarke and Park transforms), and regulates two components: iq, which makes torque, and id, which is held at zero. The stator field stays at exactly 90°, so torque is smooth and efficiency is best. It needs fine rotor position (an encoder or an observer) and fast current loops. Motors built for sinusoidal drive are usually called PMSMs.

Mechanical and electrical angles differ by the number of pole pairs p: θe = p·θm. A motor with 7 pole pairs commutates 7 times per mechanical revolution.

Steppers

A stepper advances a fixed angle per pulse, typically 200 steps per revolution (1.8°), so position can be controlled open-loop by counting pulses. Microstepping drives the two phases with sine-shaped current to subdivide steps. It smooths motion, but each microstep produces only a small restoring torque, so accuracy doesn't improve in proportion. Torque falls with speed, because the winding inductance limits how fast current can build within each step. High-voltage chopper drives (24–80 V) push that limit out. Overload a stepper and it silently loses steps, which closed-loop steppers detect. Steppers also have mid-speed resonance bands.

Servos and induction motors

An industrial servo is a motor (usually PMSM) with an encoder and a drive running current, velocity and position loops. A hobby RC servo is a geared DC motor with a potentiometer, commanded by a 1–2 ms pulse every 20 ms. AC induction motors are rugged and inexpensive, run directly from mains or a variable-frequency drive (VFD), and turn slightly slower than the field (slip).

The transmission links what the motor can do to what the load needs. One number, the ratio, sets torque, speed, reflected inertia and resolution all at once. You know gear geometry; this module looks at the transmission as part of a dynamic system.

What a ratio does

With ratio N (motor turns per load turn) and efficiency η: load torque is N·η times motor torque, load speed is motor speed divided by N, and load inertia seen by the motor is divided by N². Encoder resolution at the load improves N times, and so does stiffness against disturbances felt at the motor.

The inertia ratio, load inertia reflected to the motor divided by motor inertia, is the number servo engineers check first. Ratios up to about 5–10:1 tune easily with stiff couplings. Higher ratios work but make the axis more sensitive to compliance and resonance.

For a pure-inertia load, load acceleration is greatest when the reflected load inertia equals the motor inertia: N* = √(JL/Jm). In practice speed limits usually force a lower ratio, so pick N to cover the speed range first, then check torque and inertia ratio.

Backlash and compliance

Backlash is a dead zone inside the control loop. With the encoder on the motor, the load wanders by the backlash; with the encoder on the load, the loop hunts back and forth in a limit cycle. Fixes: preloaded or anti-backlash gearing, or dual-loop control with a motor encoder for velocity and a load encoder for position.

Compliance (belts, long shafts, couplings, strain-wave gears) joins the motor and load inertias into a two-mass system with a resonance. Keep the control bandwidth well below it, roughly one third to one fifth of the first resonance, or use notch filters.

To predict or tune a loop you need a model. Differential equations become transfer functions, and transfer functions have a few characteristic behaviors. Near the frequencies you care about, most electromechanical systems look first- or second-order.

Transfer functions in one paragraph

The Laplace transform turns differentiation into multiplication by s, so a linear differential equation becomes an algebraic ratio G(s) = output/input. The roots of the denominator are the poles. Each pole contributes a natural motion ept: real poles decay exponentially, complex pairs oscillate. Poles in the left half-plane (negative real part) decay, so the system is stable. The further left, the faster they decay.

First and second order

A first-order system τ·dy/dt + y = K·u has G(s) = K/(τs + 1). A DC motor's speed response to voltage looks like this when inductance is small: G(s) = (1/Ke)/(τms + 1).

A second-order system has G(s) = ωn²/(s² + 2ζωns + ωn²). You know it as m·ẍ + c·ẋ + k·x = F, with ωn = √(k/m) and ζ = c/(2√(km)). Its poles sit at −ζωn ± jωn√(1−ζ²). Many closed position loops behave this way.

Mechanical bridge

A proportional position loop is a virtual spring and the derivative term is a virtual damper. Tuning PD gains is choosing k and c for a mass–spring–damper you build in software (Module 12).

Step response specs

  • Overshoot Mp = e−πζ/√(1−ζ²), depending only on ζ. ζ = 0.7 gives about 4.6%; ζ = 0.5 gives about 16%.
  • Peak time tp = π/ωd, where ωd = ωn√(1−ζ²) is the damped frequency.
  • Settling time (2% band) ts ≈ 4/(ζωn), set by how far left the poles are.
  • Rise time (10–90%) tr ≈ 1.8/ωn for moderate damping.

Feedback makes a system follow commands and reject disturbances even when the model is uncertain. PID control covers the large majority of industrial loops, and in motion control it is usually PID plus feedforward.

The three terms

With error e = r − y, the controller output is u = Kp·e + Ki·∫e dt + Kd·de/dt.

  • Proportional acts as a virtual spring. More Kp gives a faster, stiffer response and less error, but also more overshoot and noise. Combined with delays and resonances, enough of it causes instability.
  • Integral keeps pushing as long as any error remains, so it removes steady-state error against constant disturbances like gravity and friction. Too much causes slow overshoot and oscillation.
  • Derivative acts as a virtual damper and adds phase lead. It amplifies sensor noise, so it is always low-pass filtered, and it is usually computed from the measurement rather than the error, so a setpoint step doesn't produce a huge spike (“derivative kick”).

Proportional control alone leaves an error against a constant disturbance torque. For the motor in the lab below, that error is ess = Td·R/(Kt·Kp): the virtual spring has to deflect to produce the torque.

Integrator windup

When the output saturates (the driver is at full voltage), the integrator keeps accumulating error it can't act on. When the error finally reverses, the oversized integral has to unwind, which causes large overshoot. Anti-windup stops integrating while the output is saturated and the error would push it further, or bleeds the integral back (back-calculation).

Feedforward

If you know the torque a move needs (J·α for acceleration, plus friction and gravity), add it directly to the output. Feedback then only corrects what the model got wrong. For motion profiles this improves tracking more than any amount of gain tuning.

A practical tuning sequence

  1. Start with Ki = Kd = 0. Raise Kp until the response is quick with modest overshoot.
  2. Add Kd to damp the overshoot. Then raise Kp further and repeat until noise or ringing limits you.
  3. Add Ki slowly until steady-state error clears in an acceptable time.
  4. Add feedforward, test with large moves to check saturation behavior, and leave margin (Module 13).

Digital implementation

// Runs in a timer interrupt every Ts seconds
float pid_update(PID *c, float ref, float meas, float u_ff) {
    float e = ref - meas;

    // derivative on measurement, low-pass filtered
    float d_raw = -(meas - c->prev_meas) / c->Ts;
    c->d_filt  += c->alpha * (d_raw - c->d_filt);
    c->prev_meas = meas;

    float u   = c->Kp*e + c->Ki*c->integ + c->Kd*c->d_filt + u_ff;
    float sat = clamp(u, -c->u_max, c->u_max);

    // anti-windup: integrate only if not pushing deeper into saturation
    if (u == sat || e*u < 0) c->integ += e * c->Ts;
    return sat;
}

Time-domain tuning by feel only goes so far. Bandwidth and phase margin tell you how fast a loop can be and how close it is to oscillating, and they explain why delays, filters and resonances limit performance.

Frequency response

Drive a linear system with a sine and the output is a sine at the same frequency, scaled and shifted. A Bode plot shows that scale (in dB) and shift (in degrees) across frequency. For a loop, look at the open-loop transfer function L(s) = C(s)·G(s): controller times plant, around the loop once.

Crossover and phase margin

The crossover frequency ωc is where |L| = 1 (0 dB). It is roughly the closed-loop bandwidth: the loop follows commands and rejects disturbances below it and ignores them above it. The phase margin is how far the loop phase is from −180° at crossover: PM = 180° + ∠L(jωc). At −180° the feedback reverses sign and the loop oscillates. Aim for 45–60°. As a rule of thumb, closed-loop damping is about ζ ≈ PM/100.

The gain margin is how much the gain could rise before the loop goes unstable, measured where the phase crosses −180°.

Delay costs phase

A pure delay Td doesn't change magnitude but adds a phase lag of ω·Td radians, growing without limit. A digital controller adds about 1.5 sample periods of delay (computation plus the zero-order hold), so a 1 kHz loop has roughly 1.5 ms. At 100 Hz that costs 54° of phase. Filters, sensor latency and PWM add more. This is why the loop rate must be well above the crossover frequency.

Cascaded loops

Servo drives nest three loops. Each inner loop is made much faster than the one around it, so the outer loop sees it as nearly ideal. Tune from the inside out: current first, then velocity, then position.

Mechanical resonances show up as peaks in the plant's magnitude. If a peak pokes above 0 dB near or beyond crossover, the loop rings or goes unstable there. Notch filters cut the peak, at some cost in phase.

Many electromechanical failures aren't control problems: brownouts, ground loops, noise on encoder lines, missing fuses. These habits make prototypes behave, and some of them keep people safe.

Power supplies

  • Size the supply for the average power plus margin, and let bulk capacitance near the driver cover acceleration peaks.
  • Plan for regeneration (Module 05). Decelerating loads push energy back onto the bus. Use the calculator below to check the voltage rise, and add a brake resistor or more capacitance if needed.
  • Give logic its own regulated supply, well filtered from the motor bus. A microcontroller that resets whenever the motor starts is the classic symptom of a shared, sagging supply or a shared ground return.
  • Add reverse-polarity protection (a series Schottky diode, or a MOSFET for lower loss) and a fuse sized to protect the wiring.

Grounding and current loops

  • Every current returns to its source. Keep high-current motor returns separate from signal grounds and join them at a single point (star ground), usually at the driver or supply.
  • Keep loop area small. Run each supply wire next to its return, and twist motor leads. Large loops radiate and pick up noise like antennas.
  • Ground loops form when two grounded devices are connected by both a signal cable and a separate ground path. Break them with a single ground reference or galvanic isolation (optocouplers, digital isolators).

Noise

  • PWM edges change by tens of volts in nanoseconds and couple capacitively into nearby wiring. Route encoder and analog lines away from motor leads, or cross them at right angles.
  • Use differential signaling over cable: RS-422 line-driver encoders, CAN, RS-485.
  • Shielded motor and encoder cables are usually grounded at the drive end. Follow the drive maker's instructions, since practice varies.
  • Decouple every IC with 100 nF close to its supply pins, and add bulk capacitance where cables enter a board.

Wire sizing

Ampacity depends heavily on the standard, insulation and bundling, so check a current-rating table for your application. Voltage drop is easy to compute, though, and often the tighter constraint on long, low-voltage runs. Remember the current flows out and back.

Safety

  • An emergency stop must remove power from the actuators through hardware (a safety relay, contactor or the drive's Safe Torque Off input), never through software alone.
  • Vertical axes fall when power is removed. Use a holding brake, counterbalance or self-locking transmission.
  • Stored energy remains after power-off: bus capacitors, springs, raised loads, pneumatics.
  • Machinery safety standards such as ISO 13849 and IEC 60204-1 govern industrial equipment. Work on mains voltage calls for qualified practices.

Sizing ties the whole course together. A mechanical move becomes a velocity profile, the profile becomes a torque profile through inertia and the transmission, and the motor and drive have to cover the peak torque, the RMS torque and the top speed.

Motion profiles

A trapezoidal profile accelerates, cruises and decelerates. For a move of distance D in time t with acceleration and deceleration each taking ta, peak velocity is v = D/(t − ta) and acceleration is a = v/ta. The common one-third split (ta = t/3) gives v = 1.5·D/t and a = 4.5·D/t². A triangular profile (ta = t/2) needs the lowest acceleration for a given time and the highest speed.

S-curve profiles limit jerk (the rate of change of acceleration). They excite resonances less and settle faster, at the cost of slightly higher peak acceleration for the same move time.

From profile to torque

In each segment, motor torque is the total inertia at the motor times its acceleration, plus friction and external loads referred through the transmission: T = (Jm + JL/(ηN²))·αm + Tf,L/(ηN) + Text/(ηN). Gravity on a vertical axis is an external load that is present even while stopped.

Peak, RMS and speed

  • Peak torque (usually during acceleration) must be below the motor and drive's peak rating, with margin.
  • RMS torque over the whole cycle, dwell included, is what heats the motor. Compare it with the continuous rating: Trms = √(Σ Ti²·ti / Σ ti).
  • Peak speed must be within the motor's rated range, and the torque available at that speed (from the torque–speed curve) must still cover the requirement.
  • Voltage: back-EMF at top speed plus R·I at peak current must fit under the bus voltage: V ≥ Ke·ωmax + R·Ipeak.
  • Inertia ratio: check it against the drive maker's guidance (Module 10).

The sizing loop

  1. Define the move and the cycle, including dwell.
  2. Choose the transmission type and a first ratio from the speed range.
  3. Compute load inertia, friction and external forces, referred to the motor.
  4. Build the motion profile and the torque in each segment.
  5. Pick a candidate motor and add its rotor inertia. This step is easy to forget.
  6. Check peak torque, RMS torque, speed, voltage and inertia ratio. Adjust the ratio or motor and repeat.
Rule of thumb

Keep about 20–30% margin on both peak and RMS torque. Friction estimates are usually optimistic, and motors are rated at a specific ambient temperature and mounting.

A shaft in a motion system carries rotating bending from belt and gear loads and torque that often reverses with every move, and it has to stay stiff enough not to limit the control loop. This module refreshes Shigley's fatigue-based shaft design, then covers the stiffness, critical speed and shaft–hub practice that matter for servo axes.

What the shaft actually sees

  • Bending is fully reversed. A transverse load fixed in direction (belt pull, gear separating force, an overhung pulley's weight) is steady, but each point on a rotating shaft passes from tension to compression once per revolution. So for fatigue, Ma = M and Mm = 0.
  • Torque depends on the duty. A conveyor or pump drive has mostly steady torque (Tm). A servo axis that accelerates and brakes in both directions sees torque that reverses with every move, so it is mostly alternating (Ta). Shigley's worked examples usually assume steady torque, so check the torque profile from your sizing (Module 15) before you copy their assumption.
  • Axial loads from helical gears or screws are usually small in stress terms but matter for bearings (Module 17).

Nominal stresses on a solid round shaft are σ = 32M/(πd³) in bending and τ = 16T/(πd³) in torsion. The alternating and midrange components are each combined into a von Mises equivalent, σ′ = √(σ² + 3τ²), after applying stress-concentration factors.

Endurance limit and the Marin factors

Stress concentration and notch sensitivity

Geometry gives the theoretical factor Kt. Material decides how much of it matters: the fatigue factor is Kf = 1 + q·(Kt − 1), where the notch sensitivity q (0 to 1) rises with strength and with notch radius. With both alternating and midrange stress, a failure line on the σm–σa plane sets the safety factor. Goodman (a straight line from Se to Sut) is the conservative standard. Rating life

Rolling bearings fail by surface fatigue, and identical bearings under identical conditions show wide scatter. The L10 life is the number of revolutions that 90% of a large group reach or exceed. The basic dynamic load rating C is the load that gives an L10 of one million revolutions. Life then follows a power law:

Basic rating life
L10 = (C/P)a × 10⁶ reva = 3 for ball bearings, 10/3 for roller bearings
L10h = (10⁶ / 60n)·(C/P)aIn hours at n rpm

The exponent makes life very sensitive to load: doubling the load on a ball bearing cuts its life by 8×. For reliability above 90%, multiply by a life-adjustment factor a1 (ISO 281): 0.64 at 95%, 0.25 at 99%. Modern catalogs also apply aISO, which accounts for lubricant film and contamination and can raise or lower life several-fold. Use the maker's calculator for final numbers once the type and size are chosen.

Equivalent load

Combined radial and axial loads are converted into one equivalent radial load P = X·Fr + Y·Fa. For a deep-groove ball bearing: if Fa/Fr ≤ e, then P = Fr. Otherwise X = 0.56 and Y comes from the catalog table, where both e and Y depend on Fa/C0. Multiply by an application factor for dynamic effects: about 1.0–1.2 for smooth machinery, 1.2–1.5 for light impact, 1.5–3 for moderate impact.

For a duty cycle with varying load, use the cubic mean weighted by revolutions, not time: Fm = (Σ Fia·ni / Σ ni)1/a. In a servo cycle most revolutions happen at cruise speed, so cruise loads dominate bearing life even though acceleration loads dominate motor sizing.

Static capacity

Slow, oscillating or stationary bearings fail by permanent dents rather than fatigue. Check the static safety factor s0 = C0/P0, aiming for at least 1 for smooth, quiet duty and 2 or more with shock or where quiet running and precision matter. For a deep-groove ball bearing, P0 = max(Fr, 0.6Fr + 0.5Fa).

Mounting: fits, location and preload

  • Fits follow the rotating load. The ring that rotates relative to the load gets an interference fit, or it will creep and fret. On a typical rotating shaft that's the inner ring (shaft tolerances roughly j5 to m6, tighter for heavier loads), while the outer ring gets a sliding fit such as H7 in the housing.
  • Locate one end, let the other float. One bearing fixes the shaft axially; the other slides in its housing (or is a cylindrical roller) so thermal growth doesn't load the bearings against each other.
  • Shoulders must be tall enough to support the ring but low enough to allow removal, and the shaft fillet radius must be smaller than the bearing's corner chamfer. Catalogs list both.
  • Preload removes internal clearance, raising stiffness and positioning accuracy, at the cost of friction and heat. Angular-contact pairs mounted back-to-back (DB, “O”) have a wide effective spread and high moment stiffness; face-to-face (DF, “X”) tolerates more misalignment.
  • Lubrication and sealing. Grease suits most motion axes; fill a fraction of the free space rather than packing it full, which causes heat and churning. Contact seals keep contamination out but add drag; shields add little drag but seal less.

Linear guides

Profile-rail guides use recirculating balls or rollers in a carriage (block) running on a ground rail. Their life follows the same law, measured in distance: L = (C/(fw·P))³ × LR, where LR is the rating distance, either 50 km or 100 km depending on the maker. The same guide rated at 50 km shows a C about 26% higher than at 100 km (21/3 = 1.26), so compare catalogs on the same basis. Roller guides use 10/3 instead of 3.

  • Moment loads and offset loads concentrate on single blocks. Use two blocks per rail and two rails where possible, and space blocks widely.
  • Preload classes trade stiffness for friction and life.
  • Mount one rail against a reference edge as the master, and align the second to it within the maker's parallelism tolerance.
  • Plain bushings on round shafts bind if the drive force is far from the bearing. Keep the ratio of the force's offset to the bearing length below 2:1 (the “2:1 rule”).
Best practices
  • Size bearings from the cubic-mean load of the actual duty cycle, with an application factor.
  • Check static capacity for slow, oscillating or shock-loaded positions.
  • Design for one locating and one floating bearing per shaft unless the arrangement is deliberately preloaded.
  • Pick fits from the catalog by load type and magnitude; don't default to a press fit on both rings.
  • For ball screws, use a preloaded angular-contact pair at the fixed end and a deep-groove bearing at the floating end.

Module 10 treated screws, belts and gears as ratios and inertias. This module covers what limits them physically: thread friction and self-locking, screw buckling and whip, belt tension and stiffness, and gear tooth bending and pitting.

Power screws

Square, Acme (29° included angle) and metric trapezoidal (30°) threads convert torque to thrust by sliding friction. With major diameter d and pitch p, the mean diameter is dm ≈ d − p/2 and the root diameter dr ≈ d − p. The lead is l = nstarts·p and the lead angle is tan λ = l/(π·dm).

Thread torque (Acme, half-angle α = 14.5°)
TR = (F·dm/2)·(l + πf·dm·sec α)/(π·dm − f·l·sec α)Raising (driving against the load)
TL = (F·dm/2)·(πf·dm·sec α − l)/(π·dm + f·l·sec α)Lowering; if positive, the screw self-locks
e = F·l/(2π·TR)Efficiency

Friction coefficients for steel on a bronze nut with oil are around 0.10–0.15; dry steel on steel is 0.15–0.25; polymer nuts are lower. Any thrust collar adds F·fc·dc/2 of torque unless it runs on a rolling thrust bearing. Self-locking is useful for holding loads without power but means efficiency is below 50%, and vibration can still make a self-locking screw creep.

Ball screws replace sliding with rolling balls: efficiency above 90%, low wear, predictable life L = (Ca/Fm)³ × 10⁶ rev, preloadable nuts for zero backlash, and accuracy grades for positioning. They back-drive easily, so vertical axes need a brake.

Belts

Friction belts (flat and V) transmit torque through the tension difference allowed by friction. At the slip limit, T1/T2 = efθ (Euler–Eytelwein), with θ the wrap angle; for a V-belt replace f with f/sin(β/2). They slip under overload, which protects the drive but rules them out for positioning.

Timing (synchronous) belts transmit force through teeth, so they don't slip and suit positioning. Pitch diameter is d = Z·p/π. The effective pull is Te = 2T/d = T1 − T2. With installation tension Ti, the tight side runs at about Ti + Te/2 and the slack side at about Ti − Te/2. If the slack side goes loose the belt can jump teeth, so Ti must exceed Te/2 by a margin; follow the maker's tension recommendation.

  • Keep at least 6 teeth in mesh on the small pulley, and respect the maker's minimum pulley size for belt bending fatigue.
  • Flange one pulley (or alternate flanges) to stop the belt walking off.
  • On a linear belt axis the carriage sits between two belt spans acting as springs. Stiffness is EA·(1/L1 + 1/L2), lowest at mid-travel where it equals 4EA/L. That stiffness with the carriage mass is often the axis's first resonance.

Gears

In SI, gear size is given by the module m = d/N (mm), so the pitch diameter is d = m·N and the circular pitch is p = π·m. Standard involute teeth use a 20° pressure angle. Below about 17 teeth, full-depth 20° pinions are undercut unless profile-shifted. The contact ratio should be at least 1.2, preferably 1.4 or more, for smooth running.

The transmitted (tangential) load is Wt = 2T/d. The separating (radial) force is Wt·tan φ, and helical gears add an axial force Wt·tan ψ. All of these load the shafts and bearings.

Bending: the Lewis equation

Lewis treated the tooth as a cantilever: σ = Kv·Wt/(F·m·Y), with face width F, the Lewis form factor Y (0.245 at 12 teeth, 0.322 at 20, 0.389 at 40, about 0.485 for a rack) and a velocity factor for dynamic load: Kv = (6.1 + V)/6.1 for cut teeth, (3.56 + √V)/3.56 for hobbed or shaped, and (5.56 + √V)/5.56 for ground teeth, with V in m/s. Face width is usually 3–5 circular pitches.

Pitting: Hertz contact stress

Surface fatigue (pitting) is set by contact stress: σc = Cp·√(KvWt/(F·cos φ)·(1/r1 + 1/r2)), with ri = di·sin φ/2 and Cp ≈ 191 √MPa for steel on steel. For hardened steel gears, pitting usually governs before bending.

The full AGMA method adds factors for overload, size, load distribution, rim thickness, geometry, life cycles and reliability. For a first pass, compare with AGMA grade 1 through-hardened steel allowables: St ≈ 0.533·HB + 88.3 MPa (bending) and Sc ≈ 2.22·HB + 200 MPa (contact).

Best practices
  • For screws, check torque, buckling, critical speed and life together; long, fast screws are usually limited by whip.
  • Support ball screws fixed at the motor end with a preloaded angular-contact pair; float the other end.
  • Tension timing belts to the maker's value and recheck after run-in; too loose jumps teeth, too tight overloads bearings and motor shafts.
  • Make the pinion harder than the gear, avoid undercut, and use a hunting-tooth ratio (no common factor) so wear spreads evenly.
  • Mount gears close to bearings and keep shaft slope at the mesh below 0.0005 rad for uncrowned spur gears.
